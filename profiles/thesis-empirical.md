# Profile: empirical thesis

A master's or bachelor's thesis that poses research questions and answers them with data the author collected or analysed: a survey, an experiment, interviews, a case study, secondary data, or a model built and evaluated.

Defaults only. Where the institution mandates something different, the institution wins.

## Structure

| # | Chapter | Does what | Budget share |
|---|---------|-----------|-------------:|
| 1 | Introduction | Establishes that the problem is real, matters to someone identifiable, and is not already solved. Ends with the research questions and the delimitation. | 8% |
| 2 | Theoretical framework and literature review | The concepts and prior findings the work builds on, and the gap it addresses. Ends with a summary table mapping source to method to finding to relevance. | 17% |
| 3 | Methodology | Philosophy of science, research design, case or data description, how the analysis was done, and the validity threats with their mitigations. | 21% |
| 4 | Analysis and results | What the data shows. Findings, not interpretation. | 30% |
| 5 | Discussion | What the findings mean, against the literature of Chapter 2. Theoretical and practical implications, then limitations. | 14% |
| 6 | Conclusion | Direct answers to the research questions, the contributions, and future research. | 10% |

Percentages of the total budget, to be adjusted to the institution's weighting of the assessment criteria. Where the analysis carries the most weight, give it the most space.

## What examiners look for

**Research questions that can actually be answered** by the method proposed, and that require analysis rather than description. "What is X?" is a background question. "Does X change Y, and under what conditions?" is a thesis question.

**A stated philosophy of science.** Many programmes require it explicitly, and even where they do not, an examiner notices when a positivist design is written up in interpretivist language. Say which position the work takes and what follows from it for the method.

**Delimitation with reasons.** Not a list of what was left out, but why leaving it out was defensible.

**Results separated from interpretation.** Chapter 4 says what happened. Chapter 5 says what it means. Mixing them makes it impossible to tell which claims rest on the data.

**A conclusion that answers the questions asked.** Written after everything else, checked against Chapter 1 word by word. A conclusion that answers a subtly different question is one of the most commonly punished failures.

## Common failures

The literature review that summarises papers one at a time without ever synthesising them, so the gap never emerges. Theory introduced in Chapter 2 and never used again. Limitations written defensively, listing everything imaginable so nothing is admitted specifically. A discussion that repeats the results instead of interpreting them. Numbers that drift between chapters.

## Onboarding questions specific to this profile

- What are the research questions, verbatim? Are they fixed or still moving?
- What data exists, or will exist, and when? Is any of it already collected?
- Which method, and does it match the questions? Can it establish causation, or only association?
- Is a philosophy-of-science section required, and has a position been chosen?
- Case company or institution — is the material confidential, and does it need anonymising?
- Was any of the analysis done before the writing started, and where does it live?
- Has ethical approval or a data agreement been needed, and is it in place?
