# Profile: reflective or internship report

A report on a period of practice: an internship, a placement, a project, or founding something. Structured around learning goals rather than research questions. Theory is a lens for understanding experience, not the object of study.

This genre is routinely written as if it were a small thesis, and routinely fails for exactly that reason.

## Structure

| # | Section | Does what | Budget share |
|---|---------|-----------|-------------:|
| 1 | Introduction | The setting, the role, how the learning goals were arrived at, and how the report is organised. | 12% |
| 2 | Learning goals and approach | The goals stated precisely, the theories used to make sense of them, and how experience was captured and reflected on. | 15% |
| 3 | Learning goal 1 | Theory, approach, what happened, reflection. | 20% |
| 4 | Learning goal 2 | Same shape. | 20% |
| 5 | Learning goal 3 | Same shape. | 20% |
| 6 | Conclusion | Reflection across all goals, what changed in the author's understanding of themselves, and what they carry forward. | 13% |

Each learning-goal section is self-contained: theory, then method, then findings, then reflection. Theory gathered into its own chapter is the most common structural criticism of these reports.

## What examiners look for

**Reflection, not description.** "I built a prototype, users found it confusing, I rebuilt it" is a narrative. Reflection asks why the first version was confusing, what assumption produced it, what the author now believes that they did not before, and what they would do differently facing the same situation. The grade lives in that second layer.

**Theory doing work.** A learning theory named and then abandoned is decoration. Applied properly, it explains something about the experience that would not otherwise be visible.

**Evidence.** Artefacts, iterations, screenshots, interview notes, metrics, feedback received. A report about practice that shows no product of that practice reads as unverifiable.

**Honesty about failure.** The strongest sections are usually about what did not work. An account where everything succeeded gives the examiner nothing to assess and is rarely believed.

**Self-knowledge.** Many programmes explicitly require reflection on how the experience changed the author's sense of their own strengths, weaknesses, and professional identity. It cannot be added in the final paragraph.

## Voice

First person, throughout. "I decided", "I misjudged", "I learned". The impersonal voice makes reflection impossible, since the whole point is what one specific person came to understand.

Concrete over abstract, always. Not "I developed an understanding of stakeholder communication" but "I learned that the technical detail I found most interesting was the part the customer cared about least, and I started every meeting differently after that."

## Common failures

Theory quarantined in one chapter. Description standing in for reflection. Learning goals so vague that nothing could count as achieving them. No artefacts. A conclusion that summarises rather than reflects. Jargon that a lay reader could not follow, in a genre where explaining the work in lay terms is usually an explicit requirement.

## Onboarding questions specific to this profile

- What are the learning goals, verbatim? Were they set before the placement or reconstructed afterwards?
- Which theories are mandated, and which chosen?
- What artefacts exist — code, decks, designs, notes, interview records, metrics — and where are they?
- Is this a resubmission? If so, the prior feedback is the single most authoritative document in the project. Where is it?
- What went wrong during the period, and is the author willing to write about it?
- Is there an oral defence, and does it require a presentation?
- Any confidentiality constraints on what can be described?
