# Profile: course paper or essay

A single-argument piece for one course: a term paper, an essay, a case analysis, a seminar paper. Usually somewhere between 10,000 and 30,000 characters, with weeks rather than months to write it.

The constraint that shapes everything is length. At this size there is no room for a survey of the field, so the paper has to commit to one claim in the first paragraph and spend the rest defending it.

## Structure

| # | Section | Does what | Budget share |
|---|---------|-----------|-------------:|
| 1 | Introduction | The question, why it matters, and the thesis statement. The claim appears here, not in the conclusion. | 15% |
| 2 | Background or framework | Only the theory the argument actually uses. Anything the argument does not use is cut. | 20% |
| 3 | Analysis | The argument, in steps, with evidence at each step. Usually two or three subsections, one per move. | 45% |
| 4 | Conclusion | What was established, what follows, and what it does not settle. | 20% |

For a case analysis, section 3 becomes the case applied to the framework, and the framework section shrinks further.

## What examiners look for

**A claim, stated early.** The most common weakness in student essays is a paper that circles a topic without arguing anything. If the thesis statement cannot be written in one sentence, the paper is not ready to draft.

**Argument, not coverage.** At this length, breadth is a mistake. One idea examined properly beats five mentioned.

**Engagement with the counter-argument.** The strongest short papers name the best objection and answer it. Ignoring it does not make the examiner forget it exists.

**Course material used.** Graders usually want to see the semester's concepts applied. Using an outside framework instead, without justification, reads as avoidance.

## Common failures

A long introduction to a short paper. Summarising sources rather than using them. A conclusion that introduces a new idea. Padding through repetition, which at this length is immediately visible.

## Onboarding questions specific to this profile

- What is the claim, in one sentence?
- Which course concepts must appear?
- Is there a prescribed question, or is the topic chosen?
- Which readings are the graders expecting to see used?
- Individual or group? If group, who owns which section?
